Part I · 02

How we read your brief

You asked four questions — name, architecture, identity, positioning. We think they are one question wearing four coats:

How much of the institution crosses the aisle?

You have eight years of genuinely rare equity: purpose-built AI for finance, clients running $5T, auditable outputs in a category drowning in confident nonsense. The consumer thesis — every institutional advantage should be available to the individual — only works if that equity survives the crossing. Your RFP claims something stronger — “one product, one job, best in the world at it.” That is a positioning of capability, not tone. Serious is a manner. Best-in-the-world-at-one-job is a claim. Part I · 04

How we’ll answer the naming question

Not a generic process description — here is the actual method, with the parts that usually kill naming projects handled first.

Disqualification before generation

Week one is spent writing the tests a name must survive before anyone proposes names — and “Boosted” goes through the same gauntlet as every candidate. If the incumbent survives, you keep it with evidence instead of sentiment; if it dies, everyone watched it die fairly. The tests are agreed with you in advance: linguistic screens, trademark class 36/42 knockouts, entity-search viability, and the perception tests below.

Names tested in context, never on whiteboards

A name that sounds distinguished in a conference room dies in a push notification. Every surviving candidate is tested where the DRI will actually meet it: an App Store listing, a 6:31 a.m. notification, a line in a Reddit thread (“anyone else using ___?”), a compliance footer. We test the incumbent in the same frames. This is where “Boosted” either clears its gaming-adjacent and hype-adjacent readings or doesn’t — measured with your actual audience, a panel of ~300 self-directed investors screened to your DRI definition, plus 12 depth interviews.

The institutional stress-test

Renames don’t fail with new audiences; they fail with old ones. We run a client council — eight to ten conversations with the institutional relationships you most need to keep — testing rename tolerance and the transition narrative (“formerly Boosted.ai”) before any decision is made. Your head of institutional sales sits inside this workstream, not downstream of it.

The domain question, answered with evidence

You asked specifically about .ai vs .com. Our position, which we’ll substantiate with your search data in week two, is in §09 — the short version: the TLD matters far less than the distinctiveness of the name itself, and “Boosted” has an entity-search problem no domain can fix.

Architecture: decided by risk, not by taste

Branded house vs. endorsed vs. separated is a risk-allocation decision: what happens to the institutional business if the consumer product stumbles publicly, and what happens to the consumer product if it launches without the institutional proof. We model both failure modes with you in week three and let the architecture fall out of the answer. The three directions in Part III correspond to the three defensible answers.

Part II · 08

The audit, started early: the name

We didn’t want to pitch you a process for answering the name question. We wanted to show you how we’d answer it. What follows is a real first pass — the work of your first three weeks, begun.

Where “Boosted” comes from — gradient boosting, the ensemble ML technique; the legal entity is Gradient Boosted Investments. For an audience of quants, it’s a sly, credentialed joke. That audience got you to $5T. The joke does not survive the aisle-crossing: the DRI will never meet the etymology, only the English word — and the English word has acquired three readings since the company was named, all of them pointed the wrong way.

Gaming“A boosted account” — a rank someone else earned for youDirectly inverts your user’s self-image. The DRI’s core identity is I am good at this myself. A “boosted” investor is a carried one.

Eight years of B2B equity is real: recall among institutional buyers, press history, an origin story that flatters exactly the people who write seven-figure contracts. If your future were institutional-first, we would tell you to keep the name and spend the money elsewhere. Your future, per your own RFP, is not institutional-first.

We believe the name will not survive contact with the Daily Reliant Investor, and we will test that belief honestly against a real DRI panel before anyone books a domain. Part II · 09

The domain question (.ai vs .com), answered straight

You asked whether a .ai domain performs comparably to a .com for SEO and GEO, and whether the domain should drive the naming decision. Straight answers:

  • Traditional SEO: effectively neutral. Google treats .ai as a generic TLD (it’s on the gccTLD list — Anguilla’s ccTLD handled as generic), so there is no ranking penalty or geotargeting constraint. Any agency telling you .com ranks better is selling you 2012.
  • GEO (answer engines): the TLD is irrelevant; the name is everything. LLM-based engines resolve brands as entities, weighting mention frequency, source authority, and — critically — disambiguity. A distinctive name gets cleanly resolved and cited; a common word gets diluted. Your GEO problem is “Boosted,” not “.ai.”
  • Where .com still pays, specifically for you: type-in trust with a consumer finance audience that skews 35–60 and affluent, resistance to phishing lookalikes (a real consideration for a product connected to brokerage accounts), and email domain credibility. Marginal, but your category is trust — marginal trust is the product.
  • The 2026 wrinkle: “.ai” has become the signature of the GPT-wrapper cohort — the companies you open your RFP by distinguishing yourselves from. For an eight-year AI company, the suffix now undersells: it makes the most defensible thing about you look like a costume everyone else is also wearing.

Recommendation

Name first, domain second — but make “clean .com acquirable at defensible cost” a screening criterion, not an afterthought. Keep boosted.ai forever as a redirect and for institutional/API surfaces during transition. Never let a domain broker’s price veto the right name; never pick a name whose only home is a hack.

Part II · 10

“Alfa”

The institutional product name is a deliberate misspelling of alpha — legible and even charming to buyers who meet it through a salesperson and a MAP document. In a consumer context it meets three problems it never had to face before: Alfa Romeo owns the word in consumer search; Alfa-Bank — Russia’s largest private bank, under Western sanctions — is an association no consumer finance product should share an App Store search results page with; and unattended, “alfa/alpha” reads in the crypto-Twitter register (“alpha leaks,” “alpha group”) that your seriousness positioning must never touch.

Verdict: Alfa has earned institutional equity in a channel where humans introduce it — keep it there for as long as it’s useful, potentially forever. Do not carry it across the aisle. The consumer product needs its own name in every direction we show you.

Cheap winThis decouples the scary decision (master brand) from the easy one (consumer product name) — they can move on different clocks.

Where it fails the crossing

  • Black + gold is the private-bank code. To the institutional buyer it says you’re inside. To the individual deciding whether this app is for them, it says members only — the RFP’s own feared reaction (“this is for hedge funds, not for me”) is partly a color story.
  • Dark-only is the terminal costume. It borrows Bloomberg’s authority but also its exclusion. A product for 6:31 a.m. at the kitchen table needs a daylight register it currently doesn’t possess.
  • Hanken Grotesk is competent and unownable — a fine workhorse that is also the closest thing in your stack to the “generic sans-serif” your RFP dreads. Nobody will ever recognize you by it.
  • The gold (#C19A6B) is low-chroma — elegant on black, muddy on white, invisible at small sizes in marketing contexts. It cannot survive as the sole brand color of a consumer product that must live on light surfaces, App Store tiles, and other people’s feeds.
  • No brand-level motion language, no iconography point of view — the system defines surfaces beautifully and says nothing about behavior. For a product whose whole promise is alive, watching, first — motion is not decoration, it’s the message. This is a genuine gap, and scoped in.

Part II · 12

The voice you have — two of them, actually

Your institutional voice is codified and good: authoritative not arrogant, specific over vague, outcome-led, respect the reader’s intelligence — with a banned-words list (“game-changing,” “revolutionary”) most brands need and few write down. Meanwhile your consumer advertising has already, correctly, begun speaking differently: “You’ve got the investing instincts. Now get the tools.” Identity-first, plain, respectful — the right instinct.

The gap: the two voices have no defined relationship. Nothing states what is constant across them (the spine) and what flexes (the register). Uncodified, the consumer voice will drift toward fintech-generic — “tools,” “smarter,” “free” — one ad-review cycle at a time, and the values you’ve just rewritten as a company will stay internal. The deliverable is one voice, two registers: a shared spine of principles, then codified institutional and consumer registers with the swap-tables your team already likes (“don’t say thesis; say the reason you bought it” is a genuinely great existing rule — that’s spine material).

Part II · 13

What must survive — the assets, and the bridge

Whatever else changes, four things cross the aisle intact:

  1. The proof. Eight years, $5T on platform, 10,000+ agents running. Nobody else entering the consumer market can say it. It must be sayable in one breath.
  2. Auditability as a consumer promise. “Every number traceable to a filing” was built for compliance officers; in a feed flooding with confident AI slop, it becomes the consumer moat: the one that shows its work.
  3. The service culture. You build workflows for institutional clients before they log in. The consumer translation — onboarding that sets up your watchlist agents for you — is a product truth the brand should claim loudly.
  4. The fear. The deepest one. Your institutional product exists because professionals are terrified of being the last to know something material about a name they own. The DRI has exactly the same fear — and none of the staff. That sentence is the bridge between your two businesses. Every direction in Part III is built on it.

Positioning territory, common to all three directions: portfolio intelligence — not a brokerage (you don’t hold the money), not advice (you don’t tell them what to buy), not news (you don’t cover the market; you cover their market). One product, one job: you are never the last to know about something you own.

Part IV · 15

Our recommendation, and what would change our minds

We’d bet on Direction 02 — the rename, the branded house, the morning ritual — with 01 held as the tested control and 03 as the priced hedge.

The reasoning, compressed: Direction 02 is the only setting of the dial that resolves every audit finding simultaneously — the three bad readings of “boosted,” the entity-search ceiling, the .ai costume, the members-only palette — while converting your real institutional equity into a proof line any consumer can parse in one breath. It is also the only direction organized around the user’s life (the morning) rather than your technology (the AI). Categories get won by brands that own a moment.

What would change our minds — decided at Gate 1/2, weeks 3–5:

  • If the DRI panel clears “Boosted” — no elevated gaming/hype readings vs. category baseline, and the entity-search audit shows a recoverable path — Direction 01 wins: never pay rename costs for a problem research says you don’t have.
  • If the institutional client council shows real contamination risk — enterprise clients or their compliance functions needing distance from a consumer launch — Direction 03 wins: shield the $5T business, let the consumer brand run hot.
  • Both are honest outcomes. We priced the gates so either can be chosen without sunk-cost pressure — the phases are killable on purpose.
