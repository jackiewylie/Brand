02

Context & Critical Observation

The core finding of this audit in one sentence: The prior 11-week engagement established institutional architecture parameters — naming migration, rollout priority, competitive positioning — but produced no professional brand execution. This project builds what was parameterized but not built.

The prior engagement (Fair Copy × Boosted.ai; kickoff Aug 3, delivery Oct 16, 2026 per delivery book metadata) produced a complete consumer brand system under the name Lede. The delivery book also addressed the institutional/professional side in structural terms: a naming migration table specifying that "Alfa" persists in-channel 12–18 months before folding; a competitive landscape map with Alfa's institutional position annotated; a three-door messaging hierarchy with a dedicated institutional retention message; and a rollout note that institutional users receive the transition first. These are architecture decisions, not design executions.

What the engagement did not produce: a professional visual identity, an enterprise trust surface, a co-branding framework, a white-label specification, professional workflow design, a security/governance page, or any institutional-facing production asset. The consumer concept launch page contains three sentences for the institutional audience — a reassurance strip, not a design system. The engagement was correctly scoped to its primary objective. The professional brand system was parameterized but not built. This project converts those parameters into an executed system.

Temporal note. This audit is dated August 3, 2026 — the same day as the prior engagement's kickoff. The delivery book appears in the repository as a completed artifact (delivered Oct 16, 2026 per its own metadata). All materials are reviewed as they exist in the repository at branch HEAD, regardless of their stated delivery dates. Where the delivery date is relevant to a constraint (see FM-07), it is cited explicitly.

This project owns the professional brand system: strategy, architecture, identity, website, product application, enterprise trust, co-branding, and white-label systems. 03

Current Brand Audit

Audit of the current Boosted.ai professional brand across all 10 gauntlet dimensions. Where live brand access was unavailable, scores are based on inferred and reconstructed materials — noted per dimension.

01 · Institutional Credibility & Trust

Score: 5.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.50  ·  Weighted: 8.25

The dark-gold costume signals insider status but cannot survive enterprise scrutiny

The near-black / bullion-gold palette reads as "private banking" to institutional buyers who encounter it through human sales relationships — a legitimate initial trust signal. But the current brand provides zero enterprise trust artifacts: no visible SOC 2 compliance documentation, no governance or audit-log design, no data-processing terms presentation, no deployment-architecture visuals. Security, governance, and compliance exist in the product; they don't exist in the brand. A procurement team reviewing a vendor cannot find these signals without contacting sales. That gap is critical at the contract stage.

02 · Professional Workflow Comprehension & Usefulness

Score: 3.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.25  ·  Weighted: 4.38

No professional workflow has been designed or communicated in any brand artifact

The headline "The AI engine behind modern investing" tells a buy-side analyst nothing about what Alfa does on a Tuesday morning before an earnings call. The brand assets produced to date (consumer launch page, identity system) show the DRI's 6:31 AM brief — not a team of analysts running pre-trade checks, not a head of research reviewing a 50-company watchlist, not a compliance officer approving a generated research report. The product's actual professional workflows — earnings review, thesis validation, pre-trade checks, portfolio monitoring, document analysis, agent governance — are entirely absent from any brand artifact.

03 · Distinctiveness & Memorability

Score: 4.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.25  ·  Weighted: 5.63

The current system has craft but no distinctive mark

The surface-stepped, shadow-free, border-free discipline is genuinely rare — competitors default to card borders and drop shadows, so its absence reads as a deliberate signal to any designer or engineer working in the system. But it isn't visible to a buyer reviewing a pitch deck at 100% zoom. The current brand has no logomark that survives at 16px or in grayscale, no motion signature, and a typography choice (Hanken Grotesk) that is industry-generic. The dark-gold palette is "institutional" but so is Bloomberg, FactSet, and every legacy fintech UI. Dark + gold does not distinguish; it classifies. The Instrument Line (v2) introduces the Step as a mark with concept-native meaning — this is the most distinctive asset in the prior work — but it was built for the consumer brand, not tested in professional institutional contexts.

04 · Brand Architecture & Naming Clarity

Score: 2.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.00  ·  Weighted: 2.50

The naming system is a documented disaster — multiple names, no resolution

Material reviewed confirms the following names in use or recently used for the same product: Alfa, Alpha, Alpha 3, Alpha Prime, AlphaHub, Ask Alpha, Intelligence. The company brand is Boosted.ai. A prior engagement renamed the company to Lede. The tiers Pro, Prime, Platform, Powered, and Retail appear in the brief but are never defined anywhere in the available materials as products vs. tiers vs. modes vs. internal vocabulary. A potential institutional client who has heard of "Alfa" from a conference, received a deck mentioning "Alpha Prime," and seen a LinkedIn ad for "Boosted.ai" cannot confirm they're being sold the same thing. This is not a brand perception problem — it is a commercial liability.

05 · Faithfulness to the Actual Product

Score: 5.0 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.00  ·  Weighted: 5.00

The voice is faithful; the visuals don't show the product

The institutional voice ("specific over vague," real banned words, outcome-led) accurately reflects the product's character. The consumer-facing materials (launch page, delivery book) show an accurate picture of the consumer product. But no professional brand artifact shows: the research home screen, the Ask Alfa interface, company deep-dive pages, agent activity monitors, source citation panels, thesis validation flows, or pre-trade check results. The professional product — which is the most sophisticated thing Boosted.ai has built — is invisible in its own brand materials.

06 · Visual Craft & Coherence

Score: 5.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 1.00  ·  Weighted: 5.50

The discipline is real; the application is consumer-only

The v2 token system has genuine craft: the surface-stepping, the typographic hierarchy (Schibsted Grotesk body / IBM Plex Mono data / Newsreader long-form), the 90/8/2 color ratio, the motion restraint. These are professional-grade design decisions. But the visual system was applied exclusively to consumer surfaces — the concept launch page, the Morning Lede UI. The dark product register has not been applied to institutional materials: research documents, enterprise security pages, partner portals, governance dashboards. The system exists; its professional applications do not.

07 · Verbal Craft & Specificity

Score: 7.0 / 10  ·  Weight: 0.75  ·  Weighted: 5.25

The institutional voice is genuinely strong and specific

This is the brand's highest-performing dimension. The engagement produced real rules: "specific over vague," the banned-words list, "coverage without gaps" as a precise claim. The hero copy ("The AI engine behind modern investing") is direct and not generic. The consumer voice ("never be the last to know") is disciplined and correct. The gap is professional-side: no messaging hierarchy for CIOs, compliance officers, CTOs, enterprise platform buyers, or partner-primary deployments has been produced. Audience-specific professional messaging was not in scope for the prior engagement.

08 · Enterprise, Embedded & Co-Branding Durability

Score: 1.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 0.75  ·  Weighted: 1.13

Complete absence — this is the hardest zero in the entire audit

No co-branding framework. No white-label specification. No "Powered by Boosted.ai" / "Powered by Alfa" design system. No partner-portal mockup. No Boosted.ai-led vs. Alfa-led vs. partner-primary vs. equal-weight lockup system. The delivery book defines "Powered by [NAME] Engine" as an architecture tier — and then produces no design system for it. When the Instrument Line is placed inside a Barclays-branded product, does the international orange survive alongside Barclays blue? When a bank's risk portal embeds Alfa, what attribution appears, in what size, in what position, on what surface? These questions have no answers in any brand artifact. This is the single most critical gap for enterprise sales.

09 · Operational Durability

Score: 4.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 0.75  ·  Weighted: 3.38

Token system is solid; operational templates don't exist

The design token file (v2.0.0) is machine-readable and well-organized — a real advantage over most early-stage brand systems. But the operational coverage is consumer-only. What does a sales deck look like in this system? An enterprise one-pager? A security review document? A conference booth? A LinkedIn ad for a fund manager audience? A customer story for an institutional buyer? None of these templates exist. A small in-house team receiving the Lede system tomorrow could not produce next week's institutional sales materials without inventing conventions from scratch.

10 · Accessibility, Technical Execution & Conversion

Score: 4.5 / 10  ·  Weight: 0.75  ·  Weighted: 3.38

Dark-only system has accessibility risk; no professional conversion path designed

The pre-Lede dark system (near-black + bullion gold) was never audited for WCAG 2.2 AA. The v2 tokens introduce a light ("brand") register and explicitly specify brand-text variants for small-type contrast — evidence that the team recognized the accessibility gap. But neither register has been formally tested. The professional conversion path ("Request access" → demo → security review → procurement) has not been designed as a brand journey. There is no enterprise trust page, no request-demo flow, no security documentation template. A potential institutional buyer who wants to do diligence on Boosted.ai before a procurement decision cannot complete that diligence from any brand-owned surface.

Morningstar Direct The most research-credentialed brand in institutional analytics — the Morningstar Rating defines category authority Morningstar's brand identity is almost entirely built around a proprietary metric (the star rating) that has become the category's measure of analytical authority. Institutional buyers trust the output because the methodology is documented, the ratings are independent, and the track record is public. This is the direct analogue to Boosted.ai's "coverage without gaps" claim — a proprietary framing of analytical quality that is specific, auditable, and verifiable. The lesson: own a metric, not a feeling. Morningstar's brand says "we scored it and this is what it scored." Boosted.ai's professional brand must say the equivalent for research coverage and signal detection. The co-branding outcome is instructive: Kensho retains a visible sub-brand identity (its name appears on specific products: Kensho Indices, Kensho Scribe, Kensho NERD) while the S&P master brand provides the institutional authority. This is the acquirer-primary / tool-secondary co-branding model — the opposite of what Boosted.ai's professional brand is building (where Boosted.ai provides authority, not absorbs from an acquirer). The lesson is the failure mode: Kensho's independent identity is largely invisible to institutional buyers who interact with it through S&P products. Boosted.ai's partner-primary co-branding tier must be designed with explicit minimum attribution to avoid the same erosion — "Powered by Alfa" must remain legible even inside a partner's portal design.
Tegus / Canalyst Expert network + consensus financial model integration — premium data with a distinctive sales motion Tegus and Canalyst (now part of the same platform) serve institutional research through two distinct but adjacent products: expert network calls (primary research) and standardized financial models (secondary research). Their brand combines premium pricing, named analyst relationships, and curated alternative data — a very different data-quality signal from Boosted.ai's continuous AI-driven monitoring. The lesson is about trust mechanics: Tegus earns trust through human experts who have signed an NDA and been vetted; Boosted.ai earns trust through cited sources and auditable AI reasoning. These are different trust architectures, and the professional brand must make the Boosted.ai model explicit — not by comparison to Tegus, but by making the AI verification story as concrete as a named expert on a call. "You can see exactly which filing this came from" is the professional equivalent of "you spoke to a former CFO."
Aladdin (BlackRock) The dominant embedded institutional platform — and the category ceiling for co-branding durability Aladdin is embedded in more institutional workflows than any single product in this landscape — over $21T in assets managed on its platform. Its co-branding model (Aladdin-led, client-branded portal overlays, full white-label for some deployments) is the reference case for enterprise embedded design. The professional brand's white-label and partner-primary systems will be evaluated against Aladdin's by enterprise procurement teams. This benchmark was absent from the audit's competitive map and must be carried as a reference into Phase 7.
Rogo AI-native financial research that targets the analyst directly — not the institution's infrastructure Rogo's brand premise is "your AI analyst" (inferred from marketing materials; requires verification against current positioning) — staffed-for-the-staffless positioning that targets individual analysts at hedge funds rather than procurement at asset managers. Its visual language is minimal and clean, distinctly not Bloomberg-adjacent. The professional brand lesson: the "analyst-first" entry point can reach institutional buyers without clearing enterprise security reviews first. But Rogo has not demonstrated at-scale institutional embedding. Boosted.ai's co-branding and Platform-tier work is what Rogo doesn't have — that is the differentiation axis to press.
Brightwave AI-generated investment research reports — a direct substitute for the analyst's document production workflow Brightwave is the most document-oriented competitor: it produces formatted, citable investment research reports from AI, directly replacing analyst drafting time. Its brand is explicitly premium — dark, typographically considered, positioned against institutional research aesthetics rather than fintech aesthetics (inferred from marketing materials; requires verification). The lesson for Boosted.ai: Brightwave shows that AI investment research can aspire to the visual register of institutional sell-side research. The risk: if Boosted.ai's professional brand also goes dark-and-premium, the two are visually adjacent. Differentiation must come from the workflow story (continuous monitoring vs. on-demand report generation) not just the palette.
Quill AI AI for earnings analysis — a point-solution competitor in Boosted.ai's highest-frequency professional use case Quill AI focuses specifically on earnings calls and regulatory filings — the exact document type that drives the highest-frequency professional use case for Alfa. Its brand is clean, function-forward, and relatively undesigned — a gap Boosted.ai can exploit. The lesson: a competitor with narrower scope has branded that scope clearly. Boosted.ai's professional brand must communicate its broader coverage and continuous nature without becoming generic. "Coverage without gaps" is the strategic counter to point-solution competitors: it is a claim that encompasses Quill's use case and positions Alfa as the platform rather than the point solution.
YipitData Alternative data and expert research — the premium data infrastructure competitor, not an AI interface YipitData (now part of Vista Equity portfolio) represents a different model: premium data as a product, analyst-curated, with a strong enterprise sales motion at hedge funds and asset managers. Its brand is deliberately understated — the product is the credential, not the design. The lesson: the most expensive products in this category often have the least designed brands because the buyer's due diligence focuses on data quality, not identity. Boosted.ai's professional brand must earn the right to a stronger identity — by demonstrating that the design reflects the product's quality and specificity, not substitutes for it. YipitData is the reference for what premium-undesigned looks like; Boosted.ai's professional brand is the argument that premium-designed is more credible, not less.

Competitor Swap Test — Applied at Audit Stage

Observation. The current Boosted.ai professional brand — dark background, clean sans-serif, "AI engine for investing" copy — passes the competitor swap test. The hero copy "The AI engine behind modern investing" could plausibly belong to AlphaSense, Hebbia, or Rogo with minimal copy adjustment. This is not a defect in the voice (which is more specific than this implies) but a gap in the visual and architectural distinctiveness. The professional brand being built must fail the competitor swap test — the identity must be untransferable.

Prior Attempt Failure Modes

Nine documented failure modes from prior in-house and engagement work. The gauntlet rules require that any repeat failure cap the score at 6.0. These must be tracked and explicitly resolved.

FM-01 · P0 · Professional architecture defined, not built

The delivery book established institutional parameters — naming migration, competitive position, messaging hierarchy — but produced no professional design execution

The delivery book (§02, inspected) contains substantive institutional work: a naming migration table specifying that "Alfa" persists in-channel 12–18 months before folding; a competitive landscape map placing Alfa in the institutional quadrant; a three-door messaging hierarchy with a dedicated institutional retention message ("Same engine, same team, sharper name. Nothing about your deployment changes but the letterhead"); and a rollout sequence that prioritizes institutional users in the transition. This is not "three sentences." It is a set of architecture parameters.

What it is not: a built professional identity system. The engagement produced zero professional design execution — no co-branding specification, no enterprise trust surface, no institutional visual applications, no professional workflow documentation, no partner-integration design, no security or governance page design. The gap is not that the institutional audience was ignored; it is that architecture parameters without execution cannot be shipped. A naming migration plan without a design system for the product being migrated is a decision log, not a deliverable.

Resolution required: Begin from the architecture parameters the delivery book established — treat them as inputs, not deliverables. The professional brand project converts those parameters into an executed system.

FM-02 · P0 · Naming architecture never resolved

Seven names for one product; tier labels defined nowhere

Alfa / Alpha / Alpha 3 / Alpha Prime / AlphaHub / Ask Alpha / Intelligence all appear in materials as names for the same or closely related product. Pro / Prime / Platform / Powered appear as tier/mode labels without definition. The prior engagement resolved the master brand name (Lede) but did not address the professional product architecture. No prior attempt produced a documented naming architecture with defined roles for each label.

Resolution required: A complete naming architecture table — company brand, product brand, tier names, mode names, partner names — with a legacy migration plan and a decision rule for which names survive.

FM-03 · P0 · No co-branding or white-label system produced

Enterprise sales cannot close without a partner-integration design specification

Neither the Fair Copy engagement nor any prior internal work produced a co-branding specification, white-label design system, or "Powered by" attribution framework. This is documented as an architecture tier in the delivery book but was explicitly deferred. Bank and asset manager procurement requires this before any embedded or Platform-tier contract can be signed.

Resolution required: A complete partner integration design system tested against at least six scenarios: Boosted.ai-led, Alfa-led, co-branded (equal weight), partner-primary (quiet attribution), partner-primary (silent attribution), fully white-labeled.

FM-04 · P0 · Lede identity consumer-first — institutional compatibility is an unresolved gating question

Whether the editorial visual language survives institutional contexts is not a design preference — it is a Phase 4 gate condition

The Instrument Line's identity system is built around editorial/journalistic metaphors that work for consumer marketing. In institutional contexts — compliance reports, security documentation, bank procurement portals, enterprise onboarding, conference proceedings — "proofreader's marks iconography" and "the STET stamp" read as designer choices, not institutional signals. This is not a "nice to have" aesthetic question. If the professional brand system is built as an adaptation of the Instrument Line and that adaptation is incompatible with bank procurement portals or security governance documents, the entire Phase 4 visual identity would need to be rebuilt. The cost of testing this assumption wrongly is a full system rebuild. The cost of testing it explicitly is a single design sprint.

This failure mode is elevated to P0 not because the outcome is certain to be negative — the Instrument Line may adapt well — but because the answer gates the Phase 4 architecture decision. A professional visual identity designed without resolving this question is built on an unknown foundation.

Resolution required: Before Phase 4 begins, test the Instrument Line's editorial elements (proofreader's marks, STET stamp, Masthead metaphors) against representative professional contexts (a security governance page, an enterprise co-branding spec, a fund manager one-pager). Either confirm the identity adapts (with visual evidence) or document that the professional register inherits only the token architecture, discarding the editorial overlay. The decision must be explicit and documented — not an assumption carried into visual design.

FM-05 · "Flare" orange proximity to Bloomberg orange

International orange is documented as unclaimed in investing — the values are close enough to require a prepared answer

The Instrument Line v2 identifies international orange as "unclaimed in investing" — this is a strategic claim. The token file (inspected) gives Flare: product register #FF5A14, brand register #FF4B00. Bloomberg Terminal's signature orange runs approximately #F5821F–#FF6C0C (eyedropper estimates from Terminal UI screenshots; not from Bloomberg design resources) depending on surface and screen calibration. At 100% saturation these are visually close — a fund manager who operates a Bloomberg terminal daily has a strong color association that Flare triggers.

The color story is genuinely distinct (flight recorder; "the instrument that must never be missed" vs. terminal interface chrome), but the differentiation is a verbal story, not a visual one. The values are too close for the brand to rely on color alone. The professional brand system must pre-load the flight recorder story into every context where the orange appears institutionally — not just in brand guidelines, but in sales materials, partnership documents, and design handoffs. "That's Bloomberg's color" is a foreseeable objection; the system must answer it without requiring a verbal explanation.

Resolution required: Design the orange to always appear with its justification (the instrument-line gesture, the "never be missed" story) rather than as a standalone color block. Test with professional investor sample — if 3+ evaluators identify it as Bloomberg without prompting, reconsider the exact hue.

FM-09 · P0 · Alfa Bank sanctions exposure — naming liability in regulated markets

The product name "Alfa" creates proximity risk to a sanctioned entity in the exact markets where Boosted.ai sells

Alfa Bank (Russia's largest private bank) is subject to Western sanctions enforced by OFAC (US), OFSI (UK), and the EU. A compliance officer at a UK asset manager or a US hedge fund encountering "Alfa" in a procurement review will run a standard entity-check against the OFAC SDN list. The product name returns Alfa Bank results in App Store searches, Bloomberg entity lookup, and common entity databases.

This is distinct from a trademark conflict. The issue is not that the two entities are legally confused — it is that a sanctioned-entity check by a compliance department, a prime broker, or a bank's vendor due diligence team flags "Alfa" in a name search and requires active disambiguation. The exact audience Boosted.ai is selling to (buy-side compliance teams, risk departments, procurement officers at regulated institutions) is trained to escalate this flag. A single delayed deal over an entity-check question is an existential sales friction for an 8-year-old company attempting enterprise scale.

This failure mode is not hypothetical. The risk scales with the product's success: the more enterprise deals close, the more vendor due diligence processes encounter the name. The professional brand project cannot carry "Alfa" forward without a documented legal opinion and a prepared disambiguation protocol for vendor due diligence submissions.

Resolution required: (a) Legal opinion on naming conflict risk with Alfa Bank in class 36/42 in US, UK, EU markets; (b) Entity-disambiguation language for vendor due diligence submissions; (c) A contingency naming plan if legal rates the risk high. See §09 (Top Problems) for escalation treatment.

Top Problems the Professional Brand Must Solve

Problem 1 · Most critical

The institutional buyer cannot complete diligence from any brand-owned surface

A CTO evaluating Alfa for a 200-person investment firm cannot find: SOC 2 status, data processing terms, SLA, deployment architecture, audit log documentation, or a security overview — from the website. This is not a "nice to have" for institutional sales. It is a prerequisite for a procurement committee meeting. The professional brand must build a trust surface that answers these questions before the sales call, not after.

Problem 2 · Most urgent (revenue blocking)

No enterprise/embedded product specification exists for sales to close

Platform-tier and embedded partner deals require a co-branding specification, white-label option documentation, attribution requirements, and a design handoff specification. None of these exist. Sales teams are building these from scratch per deal, which is expensive and produces inconsistent partner implementations that don't reinforce the Boosted.ai brand.

Problem 3 · Most damaging to credibility

Seven product names, no architecture — a buy-side analyst cannot track the brand

The naming inconsistency across Alfa / Alpha / Alpha Prime / AlphaHub / Ask Alpha / Intelligence / the Pro/Prime/Platform tiers creates a legitimacy problem. An analyst who researched "Alfa" at a conference, received a deck about "Alpha Prime," and now sees "Boosted.ai" on LinkedIn cannot confirm they're tracking the same company. This is the kind of inconsistency that leads a fund manager to choose AlphaSense instead — not because AlphaSense is better, but because the brand is legible.

Problem 4 · Most significant for long-term brand health

The company has two brands and no treaty between them

The consumer brand (Lede) and the professional brand (Boosted.ai / Alfa) are not designed in relationship to each other. An institutional client who encounters the Lede consumer brand — in a press mention, at a conference, from an employee's LinkedIn — must be able to understand the relationship immediately. If Boosted.ai becomes "Lede" for consumers but retains "Boosted.ai" or "Alfa" for institutions, the shared brand equity must flow in both directions. Currently, the consumer launch page says "formerly Boosted.ai" and the professional brand says nothing at all about the consumer product.

Problem 5 · Most distinctive opportunity

The product's actual work is more distinctive than the brand communicates

The product reads and analyzes 8-K filings, earnings transcripts, guidance language, market data, research, and email. It surfaces material changes, tests theses, runs pre-trade checks, monitors portfolios, and converts ad hoc research into repeatable workflows. It does this continuously, cites primary sources, and produces auditable outputs. This is genuinely distinctive — and none of it is visible in any brand artifact except the consumer launch page's Morning Lede demo. The professional brand has the opportunity to make the actual work of research visible — not as a product demo, but as a brand gesture.

Problem 6 · Acute naming liability

The product name "Alfa" creates active vendor due diligence friction in regulated markets

Alfa Bank (Russia's largest private bank) is subject to OFAC (US), OFSI (UK), and EU sanctions. Enterprise vendor due diligence processes at regulated institutions — hedge funds, asset managers, banks, prime brokers — run standard entity-check queries against the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list. The product name "Alfa" triggers this check. A procurement team encountering "Alfa" in a vendor submission does not know immediately whether "Alfa" refers to the registered product of Gradient Boosted Investments or to a sanctioned entity's digital presence. The disambiguation requires an active response from the vendor — a step that stalls the deal and signals operational immaturity to a compliance officer whose job is to find these risks.

This problem is not hypothetical — it is a mechanical consequence of how regulated-institution vendor due diligence works. The professional brand project must address it with: (1) a legal opinion on Alfa Bank naming conflict in class 36/42 in US/UK/EU markets; (2) a prepared entity-disambiguation statement for vendor due diligence submissions; and (3) a contingency naming plan that is ready to activate if legal rates the risk unacceptable. Leaving this to the sales team to handle ad hoc — as is currently the case — means the problem surfaces at the worst possible moment: in a live procurement review where the buyer's compliance officer has just flagged the vendor name. See FM-09 for failure mode documentation.
